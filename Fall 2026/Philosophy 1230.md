 Validity:
 - An argument is valid when the premises cannot be true whilst the conclusion is false, i.e. the premise must be necessary and related to the conclusion
	 - Validity is more about if the premise and conclusion connect than if it is correct
 - If, and only if, all premises are true, and the conclusion must be, then the argument is valid
	 - Invalid when the premises can be true but conclusion may not be
- Sound arguments are both valid and have true premises, making the conclusion true by definition

Invalidating Counter Example:
- An example that invalidates a argument by having a logically possible situation where the premises are true, but not the conclusion

Reductio ad absurdum:
to show that some statement is true:
- first assume it is not true
- show that it not being true leads to so contradiction or absurd claim
- conclude s is true

Circular Arguments: conclusion is also a premise
- Always valid
	- since conclusion must be true if premise is true
	- have no invalidating counterexamples

Tautology:
- a necessarily true statement
	- e.g. "Either I like pizza or do not"
	- always valid since conclusion is never false, so when premises are true, so is conclusion

Inconsistent premises: when premises contradict
- valid since "where all premises are true" premises contradict, require invalidating counterexample with true premises which doesnt work

Necessary Condition:
- For Y to be, X must be

Sufficient Condition:
- If X is, then Y must be

Reportive Definition - captures the correct usage of the word in daily use

Stipulative definition - assigns a new meaning of the term 

Precising definition - a type of stipulative and reportive definition that specifies an already existing definition to make it more precise and clear

Persuasive defintion - any definition that includes an emotive, positive, or derogatory meaning to a term where it should have none lexically.

Lexical Ambiguity - Some confusion with a word and its meaning, as it may have multiple meanings. like deep meaning profound or referring to physical depth.

Definiens are wide when they apply to things that the definiendum does not. e.g. chair is furniture, but a bench isnt really furniture in most cases
, too narrow when they fail to apply to things the definiendum applies to. These are not mutually exclusive, definitions can be too narrow and wide

Referential ambiguity - unclear in what is being referenced.

Syntactic ambiguity - having more than one meaning due to grammatical structure. e.g. "discussing violence on TV" could mean they are making the discussion on a television program as its medium, or they are discussing violence shown on television programs.

Vagueness - Terms that do not have clear points/boundaries to which they apply. e.g. heavy,dark,mountain,cheap,smart

Difference between vagueness and ambiguity
- They are not the same, they can overlap, they do no need to
- Ambiguity is about having multiple possible meanings
- Vagueness is about having unclear meanings

Incomplete Meaning - Words that require context or further parameter to denote what property or relation it expresses or measures
e.g. important or useful

Inappropriate use of jargon - when the target audience is unlikely to know its meaning

BS - what seems to be profound, but is nonsensical. Little content due to obscurity, yet are not nonsensical as they are grammatical and contain buzzwords


Distortion - using words that deviate from standard meaning in an mismatching context
e.g. sarcasm, emotive connotations
- Weasel words (an example of distortion)
	- ordinary meaning of a word being changed in the middle of a discussion, usually in a response or counterexample
- equivocation is to use vague ambiguous unclear languae with intent to decieve

Reification - Treating an abstract idea or property as if it were a concrete physical object
- Useful from dramatic impact in poetry or metaphors, but to be avoids when conveying information

Category mistakes - misrepresenting the category to which something belongs, or trying to apply a feature of a category to one that is not part of said category

Philosophical arguments and distortion of meaning - "Everyone is selfish, including people who help others. This is because everyone does what he or she wants to do.." In this argument it is implicitly assumed that a selfish person is to be defined as someone who does what he or she wants. But this is a distortion of the ordinary meaning of "a selfish person", which is more like "someone who wants to do only those things that are to his or her advantage." A person might want to do something in order to help other people, not because it is to his or her advantage. 

Empty content/statements - purported to provide information, but in actuality does not
- e.g. tautologies like "it will rain tomorrow, or not"
	- not good for communicating information, but useful in logic and considering options/possibilities
